"A midlife crisis...? You're only 29..." That's what my friends say most often when they hear what's been troubling me lately.

I recently read James Hollis's *The Middle Passage*. Out of the 40 books I read in 2026, it's the one that shook me the most and left the deepest mark. Here is how the author defines the "midlife crisis":

> The Middle Passage begins when the person is obliged to ask anew the question of meaning.

From a psychological-development point of view, James Hollis divides adult life into two stages:

**The first adulthood**: we are busy building a social identity, chasing work, achievement, relationships and security, trying to become the kind of adult the world expects. We keep answering the question: "Who does the world expect me to be?"

**The second adulthood**: after passing through the Middle Passage, we begin to get to know ourselves again, let go of identities and roles we built that may not truly be ours, and turn to explore our inner longings and the meaning of our lives. We begin to ask: "How does my soul truly wish me to live my life?"

The transition between the two stages is what Hollis calls **The Middle Passage**.

Honestly, the book is not an easy read. Jungian psychology is full of abstract terms, and although I technically finished it, I still doubt whether I understood even 50% of it. After I shared this, a friend asked me: "Did the book change you, or did you just happen to meet it at the right time?" I answered: "Both. It happened to reach me at the right time." Maybe if I had picked it up two years ago, I would have given up long before finishing. That's the charm of reading: the same book, read by different people, or by the same person at different stages of life, always brings different insights and feelings.

---

Back to my own story. It was probably more than a year ago that I slowly began to feel that my life was "a little off."

My career has always been smooth, mostly thanks to luck. Lucky to have caught the boom of the software industry, lucky to have met many mentors along the way, lucky to have once truly turned something I love into a job, and lucky never to have experienced a real "failure" in my career.

Then AI exploded, and anxiety about my career began to surface in my mind.

Over this past year I found myself caught in an extreme contradiction. I don't really like the recent changes and atmosphere in the tech industry. Frankly: "AI is truly powerful, but it makes me less and less happy, and I can't find the passion and drive that once pulled me deep into software development." And yet I keep following and sharing everything about AI. What is the real motive behind that? I think it's mainly anxiety in the face of uncertainty, and an unwillingness to accept that the achievements I've gathered over my career may no longer hold the same value in the future. It took me quite a while to admit that the latter was affecting me.\
\
I have published a technical book, but in the future fewer and fewer people will probably choose books to learn technology. If AI can carry out all the implementation, even code itself will be abstracted away, the need to deeply understand the underlying technology will shrink further, and the responsibilities of a software engineer will be completely overturned.\
\
I have been a speaker at several tech conferences, but today it's hard to get on stage if your topic doesn't include the two letters "AI", and I can't think of any impressive AI experience that I could stand in front of everyone and share. The invitations I receive to speak have indeed dropped sharply this year. All the signs are telling me that I'm falling out of step with the new era.\
\
To put it plainly, I seem to be afraid that the people who have always supported and followed me will gradually discover that I'm like Whitebeard in One Piece, a "remnant of the old era" with no place left on the routes of the new world, and that they'll point at me, laughing, and say: "You're past your prime." Even though I understand the spotlight effect, that in reality hardly anyone cares what I'm thinking or doing lately, the inner turmoil still plays out every day on schedule, tearing apart the confidence and sense of self-worth I spent years building.

![Image from the One Piece manga](/images/image-mwhk3u8c.png "align:center")

Still, I don't think AI "caused" the predicament I face today. It only "accelerated" an outcome that was bound to happen.

---

I especially like the "second curve" theory. Its core idea is: "**When your current life or career is still successful and growing, you should start looking for the next growth curve, instead of waiting until this one turns downward to begin your transition.**"

Looking across history, most things in the world cannot escape the fate of rising to a peak and then declining: nations, companies, physical abilities, and personal careers alike. Since there is a "peak," there must be a stretch of road after it that goes downhill, and that stretch can be frustrating.

![Diagram of the second curve concept](/images/image-mwhxmxyf.png "align:center size:75")

The hardest part of putting the second curve into practice is that we can't easily tell whether our peak still lies ahead or is already behind us. Subconsciously we believe we will keep getting better on this path. It's human nature.

Maybe the rise of AI sped up my progress along the first curve. The process wasn't very comfortable, but luckily it seems to have solved the problem above for me. I know very clearly that my peak as a software engineer has passed, and it's time to put some energy into discovering and building a second curve.

---

Looking back at the second adulthood described in *The Middle Passage*, the core task of this stage is to **get to know yourself again and find the life direction that truly belongs to you**. When we start to realize that the achievements, identities and lifestyles we pursued may not bring real fulfillment, we begin to re-examine our lives.

Maybe that's why this book shook me so much at this particular moment.

Thinking about it, I used to seem very clear about what I wanted. I wanted to be an excellent software engineer, wanted everyone to read my technical articles, wanted to publish a book, wanted to stand on the stage at tech conferences, wanted more people to know me. These goals did give me a lot of satisfaction, and they brought me plenty of positive feedback over the past few years. In the eyes of many peers, I was a "successful" person.

But how many of these things came from my true passion, and how many came from wanting approval and recognition from the outside world, or from satisfying an inner projection of what "success" means?

Do I like writing, or do I like the feeling of my articles being noticed by many people? Do I like sharing knowledge, or do I enjoy the moments of being recognized on stage? Do I truly love software development, or do I enjoy being a software engineer that society respects?

A friend once asked me: "If the things you love most right now couldn't be posted on social media, would you still do them?" I hesitated. I wanted to answer boldly, "Of course I would!" but I knew that answer wouldn't be honest. At that moment I realized I don't fully understand myself.

Over the past few years, I seem to have gotten used to confirming my worth through outside feedback: how many people clap for my articles, how many follow me on social media, how many speaking invitations I get, what I've achieved at work, what my annual salary has reached. These numbers and feedback gradually became the standard by which I judged whether I was doing well and whether I had value. Ironically, in conversations with others I would consciously or unconsciously suggest that I don't care about these things, as if I were a rare, unmaterialistic soul in a worldly world. The truth is that when these indicators began to decline, I realized I cared far more than I imagined. Even though I understand rationally that a person's worth shouldn't be decided by external metrics, and that it should depend more on the weight of the soul and the depth of connection, emotionally it's hard not to be affected by them.

---

Strangely, after I sorted through my vulnerability and fears and shared them openly without holding anything back, I felt relieved and rediscovered a pure happiness I hadn't felt in a long time. When I allowed myself not to have to be an impressive person, and allowed myself to go downhill by external standards, life seemed full of possibilities again, and the anxiety inside me calmed down.

My favorite passage in *The Middle Passage* is about "work and vocation."

"There is a great difference between a job and a vocation. A job is a means of earning money to meet our economic needs. A vocation is what we are called to do, and it requires life energy to accomplish. Feeling one's own productivity is a necessary part of individuation, and a person who does not answer the call harms the soul."

Perhaps the luckiest people are those who can combine work and vocation. As for me at this stage, I've come to recognize that "work" is, for me, simply a means of making a living. I need it to pay the necessary bills, and that is how my soul gets replenished. As for "vocation," I believe that one day it will finally call me, and when it does, I will know what I should do. What I need to do now is set out on the journey of exploring it, hoping that someday I'll be close enough to hear its voice, and resonate with it.

At 29, I have just stepped onto the Middle Passage. The journey has only just begun.
