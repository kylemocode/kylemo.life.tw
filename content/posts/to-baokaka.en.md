Legend has it that when a pet passes away, it arrives at the Rainbow Bridge, where time runs very differently from the human world. Decades here are only a few short hours over there. Maybe after a nap on the other side, your pet will get to see the familiar figure they've been longing for.

---

Our family's dog was called Baokaka.

**On 2026/09/27 at 9:20 a.m., 16-year-old Baokaka left for the Woof Planet 🪽**

When I got home and saw Baokaka, the feeling was strangely familiar, just like with my mom back then. I could feel the life force slowly draining away, bit by bit.

The English phrase "**is dying**" is actually very vivid. Some deaths happen in an instant, but more often it is a process, like an hourglass that can never be turned over again: consciousness, expression and energy keep slipping away until the moment the last grain falls and everything goes still.

What I most wanted to know these past few days was: what were you thinking? Did you know you were facing death, walking toward it? Were you scared?

While she was sick, my mom told me she wanted to try hard to live, and in her final stage she told me she wanted to leave, that she didn't want to hurt anymore. Although my heart hurt so much, at least I could understand her wish. But what about you, Baokaka? Did you want us to free you from the pain as soon as possible, or did you want to live until the very last moment, fully feeling the home you've lived in for 16 years and the family you've spent your whole life with?

After the pet funeral specialist took her away, he said to me in the elevator: "We've met all kinds of owners. Your family is quite rational."

Yes, that's very much our family's style: traditional, awkward, yet each of us working hard in our own way to express love.

My sister and I were both crying, but neither of us was willing to cry together in the living room. We each went back to our own rooms and only dared to sob quietly. I badly wanted to burst into tears, but then I thought, I've been acting cool for almost thirty years, so I might as well keep it up.

My dad scrolled on his phone and told us that birth, aging, sickness and death are processes everyone must go through, and that with merit fulfilled you'll have a good rebirth in the next life. The only time I've seen him cry in this life was at my mom's farewell ceremony. The way he usually expresses love is also subtle: "Go to bed early, don't get too tired," "What do you want to eat later? Let's go out for a meal," "Your company seems to be doing well lately, the stock went up again..." The word "love" never appears in our family's conversations, yet I have always been able to feel its presence.

Every time I face a farewell, it pushes me to lay out the things I consider important and examine them again. Compared with life and death, compared with what I take to be my mission, compared with love, do these things still matter? And if I still think they matter, what is the deepest motive behind that?

From the moment we're born we are living toward death. Besides the ending being fixed, it is also irreversible, and chances are that one day we'll all end up reflecting on the meaning of life and urgently wanting to know our own answer.

I think love will always be one of my answers.

People often say money compounds. I think stories compound too, so I choose to record my feelings, so that in the days ahead I can miss you again and again.

Baokaka! If you've already arrived at the Rainbow Bridge, have a good sleep. You don't have to wait long. One time when you open your eyes, you'll see me walking toward you.
